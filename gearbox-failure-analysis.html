Why industrial gearboxes fail—and what a documented rebuild should include

Prepared by Jimmy Demetrious · Industrial Gearbox Service · Updated October 12, 2026 · Version 1.5

A family-owned business backed by 52 years of family experience in gearbox service.

A symptom-to-evidence guide, inspection checklist and rebuild handover worksheet for maintenance, reliability and plant engineering teams.

Gearbox failure causes: what should you investigate first? Start by checking lubrication, contamination, alignment, loading and bearing condition. These are investigation priorities, not a universal ranking of causes. Connect the damage you find with operating history, oil analysis and component measurements before deciding what caused the failure and what to repair.

Key takeaways

  • A symptom is a starting point. Noise, heat, leaks and tooth damage need supporting evidence before a cause is assigned.
  • A rebuild needs a written scope. Agree on component decisions, assembly settings, test conditions and handover records.
  • Compare the installed outcome. Include engineering, freight, installation and incremental downtime in both options.
  • Resolve the conditions around the gearbox. Review lubrication, alignment and duty before returning a repaired unit to service.

Start with evidence before deciding on repairs

Build a record of what changed, when it changed and under which operating conditions. “Gearbox noisy” provides little direction. “New noise began after a jam, at the same input speed, and increases with conveyor load” gives the investigator a timeline and conditions to reproduce or compare.

Record the unit identification, ratio, mounting arrangement, driven machine, operating speed and duty. Gather the latest lubricant records, vibration trends, temperature readings and maintenance history. Identify recent changes to production rate, product, drive settings, couplings or foundations. Keep unknown information marked as unknown rather than filling gaps with assumptions.

Before opening or handling equipment: follow the plant’s isolation, stored-energy and lifting procedures and the equipment manufacturer’s instructions. This guide organizes an investigation; it does not provide operating limits or authorization to keep a damaged unit running.

Five areas to investigate together

1. Lubricant selection and delivery

Compare the lubricant actually used with the unit’s documented requirement. Record product name and viscosity grade, top-up history and sampling conditions. Check the specified oil-level procedure for the mounting arrangement. More oil is not automatically better: excessive lubricant can increase churning and heat. Do not substitute a generic temperature rule for the lubricant supplier’s data and the gearbox manual.

2. Contamination and sealing

Investigate how contamination could enter, including storage, transfer containers, breathers, seals and maintenance work. Retain a sample before changing or flushing oil when the investigation procedure calls for one. Use laboratory analysis to distinguish suspected contamination from a visual impression. SKF documents real cases where the lubricant specified, lubricant recorded and lubricant actually supplied were different. SKF lubrication-management examples.

3. Alignment, supports and connected equipment

Include the motor, coupling, driven machine and support structure in the inspection scope. Record alignment results and the operating conditions under which they apply. Ask whether thermal movement or external forces from connected equipment were considered. A repaired gearbox returned to an unresolved installation problem may remain exposed to the same damaging conditions.

4. Load and application changes

Compare current service with the original selection: starts, reversals, shock events, jams, speed range and operating hours. List the evidence available for torque or load rather than substituting motor nameplate power for a measured operating history. If duty has changed, ask for an application review before approving a like-for-like rebuild.

5. Bearings and assembly settings

Record bearing identities, observed damage, relevant fits and the specified setting method. Bearing damage may be part of the failure sequence rather than its starting point. Treat electrical erosion as one possible investigation path where the drive arrangement and physical evidence justify it, not a default explanation for every variable-speed installation.

Photograph parts before cleaning and follow the investigator’s handling instructions. Label component position and orientation. Keep fracture surfaces and debris available for examination. Do not reprofile, grind or otherwise remove material simply to improve appearance; the proposed process needs to account for tooth geometry, material, heat treatment and remaining capacity.

What should a documented gearbox rebuild include?

A useful rebuild scope defines inspection, component decisions, assembly settings and acceptance evidence. The following is a buyer’s checklist to agree with the repair provider, not a claim that every workshop performs every item.

  1. Intake record: nameplate, application, symptoms, lubricant information, as-received photographs and customer instructions.
  2. Evidence-preserving teardown: component identification, orientation records and an agreement on retention of failed parts and debris.
  3. Inspection plan: what will be measured, which drawings or limits will govern, and whether specialist examination is required.
  4. Condition report: findings for gears, bearings, shafts, fits, housing, seals and relevant accessories. Separate observations from suspected causes.
  5. Written component disposition: reuse, repair or replacement for each relevant part, with reasons and any remaining uncertainty.
  6. Approved repair scope: parts, machining or other processes, excluded work, lead time and change-approval arrangements.
  7. Assembly record: applicable settings and measured results, such as bearing setting, backlash and contact checks, with the controlling specification identified.
  8. Defined test: speed, load if any, duration, lubricant, measurement locations and acceptance limits agreed in advance.
  9. Handover package: test results, parts list, installation instructions, corrective actions and stated warranty terms.
  10. Return-to-service baseline: operating readings and follow-up checks agreed with the plant after installation.

Ask what “tested” means. A no-load shop run does not establish full-load performance in the plant. Record what the test demonstrated, which operating conditions it did not reproduce and what commissioning checks remain. Manufacturer instructions remain controlling; for example, Flender’s assembly and operating instructions include unit-specific commissioning requirements.

Questions maintenance teams ask

What is the most common cause of industrial gearbox failure?

There is no single defensible ranking for every gearbox and industry. Investigate lubrication, contamination, loading, bearings and installation conditions against the unit’s history. Avoid presenting bearing failure statistics as gearbox failure statistics.

Should every bearing and seal be replaced during a rebuild?

The written rebuild scope should state the replacement policy. Many comprehensive overhauls include new bearings and seals, but the word “rebuild” alone does not define what is included. Any proposed reuse needs documented inspection and an approved technical basis.

Can a pitted gear be reused?

Possibly, but appearance alone is insufficient. The decision depends on extent, progression, geometry, duty and applicable acceptance criteria. A reuse decision should identify the evidence and the responsible reviewer, not simply say “looks acceptable.”

Will changing the oil fix gearbox noise?

It may address a lubricant problem, but it will not reverse existing mechanical damage. Preserve useful samples and investigate the noise before assuming an oil change has resolved the cause.

Does passing a shop test prove the gearbox is ready for every operating load?

No. A test supports conclusions only within its conditions and acceptance criteria. Ask for speed, load, duration and recorded results, then define the remaining installation and commissioning checks.

What should I send with a gearbox repair inquiry?

Send the nameplate, application and mounting details, symptom timeline, lubricant information and available photographs or trend data. Include the required return date and ask how missing drawings or specifications will be handled.

Why can a rebuilt gearbox fail again?

Potential explanations include unresolved application conditions, incorrect component or assembly decisions, contamination, installation problems or a different failure mechanism. Compare both failure records before concluding that the same cause recurred.

Is rebuilding always faster or cheaper than replacement?

No. Parts availability, inspection findings, machining, installation changes and production constraints determine the comparison. Obtain written schedules and comparable installed-cost estimates for both options.
