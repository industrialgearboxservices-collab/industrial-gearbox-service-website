MRO Supply Watch: Mining Conveyor Gearbox Features Buyers Should Verify

Heavy conveyor and bulk-handling drives operate under sustained duty, shock loading, abrasive dust, and heat. A current manufacturer overview of mining gearboxes highlights how gearing, sealing, cooling, and mounting choices vary by application—details buyers should verify against the actual conveyor before selecting or replacing a drive.

Source note: This is an original industry brief based on the product information published on TANHON’s mining solutions page. That page describes product configurations; it does not announce a newly launched gearbox line. No affiliation with TANHON is implied.

TANHON’s public mining page describes gearboxes for mining and bulk-material applications, including conveyor, crusher, bucket-elevator, and feeder examples. It also shows that a product family can include different shaft arrangements, ratios, cooling options, and mounting configurations. The useful takeaway for an MRO team is to compare the specified model and build—not rely on a broad “mining duty” label.

What should a mining conveyor gearbox match?

Start with the conveyor’s operating profile: load, required speed, starts and stops, incline, duty hours, and known shock events. Then compare those conditions with the motor data, reduction ratio, required output torque, and the supplier’s application factors. This is especially important for a conveyor that runs continuously or experiences loaded starts; a gearbox described as heavy-duty still needs an application-specific selection check.

How do sealing and cooling choices affect mining drives?

Abrasive dust, heat, and lubricant contamination are practical concerns in mines. Manufacturer descriptions may reference taconite or labyrinth seals, cooling fans, or forced lubrication, but those options depend on the exact configuration. Confirm which features are actually included in the quoted model and ask for the operating limits and maintenance instructions. A webpage’s feature list should not be treated as proof that every unit has every option.

Why verify the gearbox configuration and interfaces?

Right-angle and bevel-helical units can differ in ratio, shaft orientation, mounting, hollow or solid output, and accessory arrangement. These differences affect how a drive fits the conveyor, base, coupling, backstop, and guarding. Before a replacement outage, compare the nameplate and dimensional drawing for the installed gearbox with the drawing and bill of materials for the proposed unit. Resolve interface changes with the responsible engineering team.

Our MRO take: compare the complete drive package

For maintenance supervisors and purchasing teams, a useful comparison includes application duty, gearbox ratings, mounting dimensions, sealing and lubrication details, cooling, access for routine service, and spare-parts documentation. A failure can interrupt material movement and production, but the financial impact depends on the specific operation; this brief does not assign a universal downtime cost.

Before requesting a quote, collect the existing unit’s manufacturer and model, motor power and speed, required output speed or ratio, mounting position, operating schedule, and site conditions. Add photos and drawings when available. Ask each supplier to identify assumptions and confirm the exact configuration being offered.

Buyer’s quick-check list

  • Record conveyor type, duty cycle, load pattern, incline, and start frequency.
  • Confirm motor data, ratio, output speed, and required torque with the supplier.
  • Match mounting, shaft arrangement, dimensions, coupling, and backstop interfaces.
  • Verify which seals, cooling, and lubrication features are included on the quoted build.
  • Review service access, maintenance instructions, and replacement-part identification.
  • Have qualified site personnel resolve application and installation questions before purchase.

This brief summarizes published product information and general comparison questions. It does not establish gearbox suitability, approve a design, or replace the manufacturer’s documentation and a qualified engineering review.
