Don’t know the brand? Find and photograph the nameplate

Start with the gearbox housing. Check the top and both sides, machined pads near the mounting feet, and areas near the input and output ends. A motor often has its own separate plate: photograph it too, but do not treat motor data as gearbox identification. Exact plate location varies by model.

  • Take a sharp, straight-on close-up and a wider photo showing each plate’s location.
  • Capture brand, complete type/model, serial/order number, ratio, speed, power or torque, mounting position, and lubricant data when present.
  • If there is no readable plate, photograph the unit, shafts, feet, motor, and connected machine; include dimensions and old drawings if available.

Frequently asked questions

Can you repair any gearbox brand?

Brand alone does not establish that a particular model or job is suitable. Share the gearbox identification, application, and photos so the proposed scope can be reviewed before shipment.

How do I identify an old gearbox with no nameplate?

Photograph the full unit, shaft ends, mounting feet, motor, and every remaining mark. Include dimensions, application, speed, ratio if known, and any drawings or prior service records. Do not guess a model from appearance alone.

Is the gearbox nameplate the same as the motor nameplate?

No. A geared motor may carry separate motor and gearbox identification. Photograph both plates and label which component each plate is attached to.

Do symptoms in the table prove which part failed?

No. Noise, heat, vibration, and oil leakage can have multiple causes. Linked manuals describe possible causes for covered models; inspection and operating context are needed to diagnose an individual unit.

Should I repair or replace the gearbox?

Compare inspection findings, gear/bearing and housing/shaft condition, parts and drawing availability, application fit, downtime, and the cost and lead time of a correctly specified replacement.
